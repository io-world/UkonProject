How I framed it

Agents don't skip cyber because it's hard to sell. They skip it because getting one client from "no cyber" to "protected" is a research project, and the premium (and commission) is small for the effort. So I treated this as a throughput problem: remove the agent's manual work at each stage, and keep people only where judgment or liability sits. The owner is the second user. They aren't technical, so everything they see leads with price and uses their own industry's examples.

I prototyped UKON's Stage 1, getting a client indication-ready, because it's the gate: nothing downstream happens if the five fields have to be hand-collected. I then carried one real client, Sloan's Lake Dental, through the later stages using only its public data to show where that output goes. In the app I split that stage into confirm data and get indications, so a person approves before client data goes to carriers, and I moved the detailed security questions after the owner has seen a price, into a questionnaire stage of their own, with the owner's decision, firm quote, bind and after-bind plan as the last stage: six stages for UKON's four. The one rule underneath it all: the database owns the numbers; the wiki owns the evidence. AI reads, extracts and explains; it never writes a figure a client sees.

Key assumptions

  • Agencies can export renewals and client records from their agency system (name, address, email domain, workers' comp headcount or payroll).
  • Carriers will price an indication from the five fields, and some may accept a revenue band.
  • Indications come back through a mix of APIs, portals and email.
  • A licensed producer presents and advises. AI drafts and explains but never advises on coverage.
  • Owners will do a 10-minute call or answer a short link, but won't read a long form.
  • Headcount stands in for full-time staff and email accounts until confirmed on the call.
  • In the prototype: the client is a real Denver dental practice and everything public about it is real (state registry, NPI, website, DNS); the agency, the calls and the carriers are simulated; the benchmarks and news items are real and cited.

My trade-offs

I prioritized the whole workflow over finished features. Every stage, from "renewal coming up" to a bound policy and the plan for the year after, works end to end for one client, so the journey can be followed the way an agent would live it. Many features behind those stages are deliberately shallow. The exception is UI and UX: where a screen needed something for a person to understand it or move through it, an infographic or navigation, I built it. In short, I prioritized the user's journey through the application over depth in any one feature.

Built, because the journey needed it

  • Stage tabs with a status worked out from the data, so the next step is always visible
  • A compare-carriers chart and terms grid, with follow-ups on each carrier's card
  • A jump menu on the agent brief
  • Questionnaire filter (not answered / answered), collapsible sections, and answers shown with their source
  • The firm quote next to the indication, with the carrier's reason
  • The 30-60-90 view and the touchpoints to renewal
  • The workflow map and the agent system map

Left shallow or simulated, on purpose

  • Carrier integrations: replies are files in an inbox
  • E-signature and the carrier's binder
  • One client: no renewal queue, sign-in or agency separation
  • No in-place refresh when stale prices are flagged; the re-request lives in Stage 2
  • Application questions modelled on common carrier forms, not UKON's carriers
  • After-bind owners proposed, not confirmed

The cost: anyone can pick a feature and find where it stops. The benefit: the whole workflow can be seen and judged, and that's the decision UKON has to make before investing in any single feature.

What I'd build first, and what I'd skip

Build first

  1. Process-mine the real workflow. Off-the-shelf mining, plus an AI layer that turns emails, carrier documents and call notes into events linked to their source. Tag why each manual step is manual: relationship, judgment, missing data or habit.
  2. Model how the main carriers work. The two or three that write most of the business and cause the most friction: how indications come back and how fast, what each application asks, appetite, conditions to bind, renewal checks. Capture what people already know before building integrations.
  3. Automate the paperwork, augment the relationships. Rank leaks by deals lost times cost to fix. Automate missing-data and habit steps; build what prepares people for relationship and judgment steps. Measure each release against the same event log and re-rank.

Deliberately skip (for now)

  • Real binding, payment and e-signature until carriers are integrated (simulated in the prototype)
  • A security scan of the client's domain; a rough, explainable exposure estimate is enough to start
  • Recalculating premiums when an agent edits; flag drift instead
  • Any AI answer about what's covered

Questions I'd still want answered

  • How do carriers return indications today (API, portal, email), and which carriers matter most?
  • Do carriers accept a revenue band?
  • What does an agency's system actually hold for a renewing client, and will agencies share an export?
  • Which step do agents say kills the deal: gathering data, explaining cyber, or the application?
  • How many clients per producer renew each month, and how many get offered cyber today?
  • Where does a CPL's time go for each bound policy?
  • Does Wispr Flow capture phone calls, or only meetings and dictation?

How I used AI to build this

Claude was my design partner and builder: framing the stages, research with sources, most of the code, and a deliberate critique of my own design. That critique called the first version "a platform, not an MVP," and I cut it to one client, built deep. The core design decisions are the guardrails around the AI: the no-digits check, the hedge override, locked carrier numbers, and escalation everywhere. The AI does the work; the system decides what to trust.
