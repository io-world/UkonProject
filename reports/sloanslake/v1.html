Operating costs (OPEX)$3,900–$4,200a day to keep the practice running, open or closed

Staff, rent, supplies and loans keep costing money while systems are down. That's about $1.41M–$1.53M a year, based on typical dental overhead of 60–65% of revenue.

What's OPEX, and how is this calculated?

OPEX (operating expenses) is what it costs to keep the doors open: staff pay, rent, supplies, lab fees, software and equipment leases, loan payments. Unlike revenue, it doesn't stop when systems go down.

$2,350,000 annual revenue × 60% to 65% typical dental overhead = $1,410,000 to $1,528,000 a year
÷ 365 days = $3,900 to $4,200 a day (rounded)

Every calendar day, because rent, salaries and loans keep running on days the office is closed. The revenue is the figure your agent has on file; the overhead share is an industry average, not your books, so your accountant can give the real number. This is a cost to you, not a statement of what any policy pays.

Your exposure snapshot

LowerModerateElevated

Every email login is a door a criminal can try. Sloan's Lake Dental has about 12 of them. Your email already runs through an encrypted service and only approved servers may send as you, which is good. But your domain has no DMARC policy, so other mail systems aren't told to reject email pretending to be you, and you never hear about it. That doesn't mean you've been hacked; it means you'd be easier to impersonate than you need to be.

  • Handles patient health information
  • 12 email accounts that could be phished
  • No email spoofing protection (DMARC) record
  • Sender policy (SPF) ends in '-all'
  • Takes payments online
  • Holds more than 5,000 records
  • Phone codes on every email login (from intake)
  • Attack-monitoring software on every computer (from intake)

Practices like yours, in the news

Longmont, Colorado dental practice reports a network breach

Artistic Smiles in Longmont found unauthorized access to its network between June 14 and June 21, 2026. It reported 3,735 affected individuals to HHS; names, addresses, birth dates and dental and medical treatment information may have been accessed.

This is a Front Range dental office, up the road from Denver, hit this summer.

Source: Becker's Dental Review, 2026-10-20

Philadelphia dental practice locked out by ransomware

In November 2025 an attacker got into 360 Dental's internal server and locked its files. About 11,273 patients were notified; names, birth dates, insurance details and treatment records were involved. The practice replaced computers, rebuilt the server, and added multi-factor authentication and VPN-only remote access afterward.

The fixes this practice made after the attack (phone codes on every login, remote access only through a secure connection) are the same things insurers ask a practice like Sloan's Lake about up front.

Source: Paubox, 2026-02-16

23 dental data breaches reported so far in 2026

A September 2026 roundup lists 23 dental breaches this year, from single-office practices to multi-location groups: from about 500 to 45,853 patients affected. Causes include ransomware, network intrusions, and a compromised account used to reach patient records.

Most of these were single-office and small group practices, not hospitals.

Source: Becker's Dental Review, 2026-09-24

Most healthcare email breaches hit organizations without DMARC enforcement

Paubox's 2026 Healthcare Email Security Report analyzed 170 email-related healthcare breaches from 2025: 74% of the breached organizations either lacked DMARC or used it for monitoring only. DMARC stops others from sending mail as your domain, but not look-alike domains or a hijacked mailbox.

Sloan's Lake already uses Paubox for encrypted email and a strict sender list, but its domain has no DMARC record, the gap this report keeps finding in breached practices. It's a free DNS change.

Source: Paubox, 2026-09-01

Phished staff email accounts behind a run of 2026 dental breaches

An Indianapolis pediatric dental practice had an employee's email account taken over after the employee answered a phishing email (5,900 people affected). A vendor whose email accounts were phished exposed patients of four dental practices in Virginia and North Carolina, and several Washington and New York practices reported unauthorized email access.

Every Microsoft 365 mailbox at the practice is a door like these, which is why insurers ask about phone codes on email first.

Source: The HIPAA Journal, 2026-06-10

Dental billing vendor reports a security incident

eAssist, a dental billing service, notified customers in September 2026 of a possible information security incident after a ransomware group claimed access.

Sloan's Lake's patient data also sits with outside vendors: Adit for booking and bill pay, Paubox for email, its website and marketing firm, and its IT provider. A problem at a vendor can still become the practice's notification duty.

Source: Becker's Dental Review, 2026-09-24

Proposed HIPAA rule would make MFA and encryption mandatory

A proposed update to the HIPAA Security Rule would require multi-factor authentication for access to patient-data systems and encryption of that data. It is not final; the final rule is currently projected for July 2027.

Turning on phone codes for every login now gets ahead of the rule and is already an insurer requirement.

Source: ComplianceHub, 2026-08-27

Ransomware and extortion

Lost income while you're down

Replaces income you lose while your systems are down, after a short waiting period of hours.

Tricked into sending money

Covers money sent to a criminal after a fake email or invoice. This usually has a smaller limit than the rest of the policy — see your option.

Lawsuits from patients

Pays legal defense and settlements if patients sue over their exposed information.

Common questions

"We're too small to be a target."

Most cyber claims come from practices like yours, not big hospitals. The attacks are automated and look for weak email setups, and Sloan's Lake Dental hasn't yet told the world's mail systems to reject email pretending to be it.

"Our IT company handles security."

Your IT company makes an attack less likely. This pays for what happens if one gets through anyway: the forensics, the patient letters, the lost days, and any lawsuits. Your IT contract doesn't cover those.
